## Week 2:

The main goal at this stage is come up with ideas to build a relevant _research question_, an _answer_ -- which you probably have the tools to address, at this point -- and a _positioning_, or _angle_. To quote the advice from [David Romer](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~jmarchan/8%20-%20David%20Romer's%20Out%20in%20Five%20Rules.pdf): 
- Have *three new ideas* a week while you are getting started.
- Don't try to game the profession, *work on what interests you*.
- Good papers in economics have *three characteristics*:
A *viewpoint*. A *lever*. A *result*.


To address that last point, we will use the RAP Framework, see [The Little Book of Research writing](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1974673162). In a nutshell: 

The **RAP framework** (Research, Answer, Positioning) is a structured approach used to define the core argument of a academic paper. The **positioning statement (P)** specifically establishes your paper's unique space within the existing literature.

### The RAP Framework Components
* **R (Research Question):** What specific question or puzzle does your study address?
* **A (Answer):** What is your main finding, solution, or takeaway conclusion?
* **P (Positioning Statement):** What is worth knowing that is still unknown, showing how your work contributes to and makes space in the current literature.

### How to Use the Positioning Statement (P)
* **Build the Introduction:** Split your single-sentence positioning statement into two contrasting claims to draft the first paragraph of your introduction.
* **Create Tension:** Juxtapose what is currently assumed or known against what is missing or misaligned in the field.
* **Provoke the Research Question:** Use that contrast to logically introduce your research question and signal your paper's distinct contribution.
* [Note from Thomas] as a result, it is more *P* _then_ *R* then *A*. In my opinion, it is useful to carefully think about *P* before _building_ *R*. Indeed, if there is no space in the literature, or if there is no tension that would ever lead to the research question, then maybe the research question is not worth pursuing. 
* [Note from Thomas] To come back to Romer's advice: the _viewpoint_ is a clear and new way of seeing an economic issue—your central claim or mechanism. It answers: What should readers understand differently? In that sense, the view point is both the positioning -- that creates a tensions -- and the question that try to tackle that tension. The _lever_ is the way you will address the question, to make progress on that claim. In empirical work, this is often credible variation, an institutional comparison, or an identification strategy. In theory, it may be a model or simplifying contrast that isolates the mechanism. It answers: Why can we learn this from your analysis? The result is the finding from using the lever -- estimate, theoretical proposition, or quantitative conclusion.

## Week 4:


**Tasks** _Due before the Monday's lecture November 30, 2026_ 
- After conversation with advisors and reading more of the literature, review and refine your R.A.P. and outline, the total outline should start looking like a proposal, up to 2 or 3 pages.  
- Use the points below when reviewing/refininig your outline. These are questions for a finished paper and I provide them just for reference. 


**The Question**
- Is the topic clearly explained? Could the question be made more precise? 
- Does the author do a good job of motivating the question in the introduction?
- Is the answer to the question obvious in advance?
- Is the question original? What is the contribution of the paper? Does the author pose a question of reasonable scope (i.e., can they reasonably hope to answer the question in a short empirical paper)? 

**The Model**
The model need not be a formal structural model of optimizing behavior, but if it is a reduced form model, there should at least be a clear verbal description of the economic theory that is behind its specification. 
- Does the model formalize the argument given by the author in the question? 
- Does the model incorporate those aspects of reality that the author seems to think are important?
- Is it possible to answer the question posed by the author within the context of the model?
- Is the model elegant? Is it simple? If it is not simple, is it unnecessarily complex? Could the author attack the problem with a simpler model?
- Is the notation clean and intuitive? 
- Is the model internally consistent? 

**Link to Empirical Analysis**
- Is the estimating equation clearly related to (or preferably derived from) the model? 
- Does the disturbance term have an interpretation within the model, or is it just tacked on? 

**The Data**
- Does the author present a clear description of the data? 
- Does the author's choice of a dataset seem well suited to answering the question he poses? 
- If you had to replicate the author's study five years from now, is there sufficient information in the paper about the source of the data and sample used in estimation that you could do it?
- Does the author discuss issues that may affect her estimation strategy: Is the data from a random sample? What are known sources of measurement error? If a panel, is there reason to believe that there may be cross-sectional dependence? 
- Does the author present summary statistics, and make good use of them to motivate the question or some specific aspects of her analysis 

**The Econometric Analysis**
- Are the econometric techniques well suited to the problem at hand? 
- What are the properties of the estimators employed by the author? Are the issues regarding these properties adequately addressed in the paper? 
- Is the econometric analysis carefully done and reported? 
- Have alternative specifications been tried and compared, when necessary? - Is the issue of robustness of the results addressed? 
- What test statistics does the author employ? Do they answer the question?
